Finance concepts explained
Annual Percentage Rate (APR) is the stated yearly interest rate without accounting for compounding. It's the number lenders advertise — but it understates the true cost whenever interest compounds more than once per year. Two loans can have the same APR yet different actual costs simply because one compounds quarterly while the other compounds monthly. Always look past the APR.
Effective Annual Rate (EAR) = (1 + APR/m)^m − 1, where m is the number of compounding periods per year. EAR captures the full compounding effect and represents the true annual cost of borrowing. When comparing loans, always compare EARs — not APRs. A 6.5% APR compounded monthly has an EAR of 6.70%; the same APR compounded quarterly yields only 6.65%.
More frequent compounding means interest accrues on interest sooner, raising your true cost. With monthly compounding, interest is added 12 times a year — each month's balance grows before the next calculation. Annual compounding only adds interest once. The more frequent the compounding, the higher the EAR relative to APR. For a borrower, less frequent compounding is cheaper (for the same APR).
An amortized loan has fixed periodic payments that fully repay both principal and interest by the end of the term. Each payment covers that period's interest first; the remainder reduces the principal. Early payments are mostly interest (because the outstanding balance is large); later payments shift toward principal. The formula is PMT = [r × PV] / [1 − (1 + r)^(−n)], where r is the periodic rate, PV is the loan amount, and n is the total number of payments. This simulator uses that formula exactly.
A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. When you borrow, you receive today's dollars but repay with future dollars. The total interest paid is the price of that privilege. A shorter term means fewer compounding periods — less total interest but higher monthly payments. A longer term lowers monthly cash outflows but dramatically increases total interest paid.
